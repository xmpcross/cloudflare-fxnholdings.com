AI helps us prepare content, but it does not decide what we publish. At FXN Holdings, we use it to support research, draft wording and enrich content such as product titles and descriptions. A person reviews AI-assisted content before publication.

That distinction matters because a fluent sentence can still be wrong. A useful publishing process needs more than a tool that writes quickly: it needs reliable source material, a clear reader need and someone responsible for deciding whether the result is ready.

Our [Technology & AI page](/technology/) explains where AI fits within the systems supporting our websites. This article explains the editorial principle behind that work and gives practical examples of the checks it requires.

## Where AI can help

Content work often involves turning uneven source material into something easier to read. A supplier may provide short specification fields, an article may need a clearer outline, or several records may use different names for the same kind of information. AI can help prepare a first version for a person to assess.

For product content, that might mean organising supplied features into a readable description. For an explanatory article, it might mean suggesting questions the draft has not answered. These are useful starting points, provided the output remains connected to evidence.

AI assistance does not make every suggested detail suitable for publication. A model can add an appealing benefit that the source never established, or suggest an answer that depends on circumstances it has not considered. The time saved in drafting should make room for careful review.

## What human review needs to establish

Our published approach keeps the decision to publish with people. To make that decision meaningful, reviewers need to consider three separate questions.

**Is it supported?** Names, specifications, dates and other factual claims need a source. If a sentence goes beyond that source, it needs further evidence or a narrower claim. Rewording an unsupported statement does not make it accurate.

**Is it useful here?** A paragraph can be correct but irrelevant to the page. A comparison reader may need a limitation or compatibility detail, while a general introduction may need a simple explanation. The audience determines what earns its place.

**Does it represent the evidence fairly?** Important conditions should travel with the claim. A feature available on one model should not become a promise about an entire range. A possible benefit should not become a guaranteed outcome.

These questions provide a practical review framework. They do not imply that every FXN platform uses identical software or an identical approval interface.

## An example: a draft that adds too much

Consider a fictional supplier record for a small desk lamp: “USB powered; adjustable brightness; adaptor sold separately.” An AI draft might describe it as “a rechargeable lamp you can use anywhere”. The sentence reads well, but it introduces battery operation and portability that the record does not support.

A more useful description would retain the supplied facts: “A USB-powered desk lamp with adjustable brightness. A suitable power adaptor is required and is sold separately.” If the exact power requirements are unknown, the reviewer should request that information rather than invent it.

The lesson extends beyond product descriptions. An article draft can also turn a tentative observation into a certainty, confuse two organisations, or describe a planned feature as already available. Review must address the underlying claim, not just spelling and tone.

Our guide to [checking AI-written product descriptions](/insights/checking-ai-written-product-descriptions/) provides a more detailed checklist for catalogue content.

## Shared content makes careful review more valuable

Our content and comparison websites use shared foundations, while individual websites retain their own presentation and releases. Reusing a record can reduce repeated work, but it also means a mistake may reach more than one page.

When reviewing shared material, consider where it will appear. A short title must still identify the right item. A description should not rely on context that only exists on one website. A missing field should not quietly become an invented fact in a generated paragraph.

The same reasoning applies to corrections. If a mistake originates in a source record, editing one visible page may leave the original problem available for reuse. Our article on [shared content without identical websites](/insights/shared-content-without-identical-websites/) explores those boundaries in more detail.

## What readers should expect from AI-assisted pages

Readers need a useful answer, accurate context and a clear way to question a claim. Producing more text is not itself an improvement. Sometimes the right editorial decision is to shorten a draft, combine overlapping sections, or hold a page until better information is available.

[Google's guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) similarly calls for accuracy, quality and relevance, including checks on titles, descriptions and other metadata. It warns that generating many pages without adding value may violate its spam policies. AI use alone is therefore not a substitute for editorial judgement or a reason to expect search visibility.

We welcome clear expectations around accountability and privacy. Our position is that using AI should leave responsibility visible: people remain responsible for the content they choose to publish. This article describes that position; it is not a claim of certification or a guarantee that mistakes cannot occur.

## Questions and corrections

If you find a statement on an FXN page that seems inaccurate, [contact our team](/contact/) with the page address, the specific wording and any source that explains the issue. That gives us something concrete to investigate.

For a content or platform enquiry, tell us what information you need to publish, where it comes from and who will use it. Those details help establish whether AI assistance would be useful and where human judgement needs to remain central.
