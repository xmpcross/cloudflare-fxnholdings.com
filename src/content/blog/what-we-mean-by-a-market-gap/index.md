A market gap is an unmet or poorly served need for a particular group of people. It might be difficult to compare suitable products, find information relevant to a region, or complete a small task without unnecessary steps. The opportunity is to make that task meaningfully easier.

At FXN Holdings, “find the gap” describes the starting point for exploring a platform idea. It does not mean that every empty search result is a business opportunity, or that a market must have no competitors. Often, the useful question is why existing options leave a specific audience doing extra work.

## Describe the problem before the website

“Build a comparison website” describes a format. It does not identify the problem the website will solve. A stronger starting point names the audience, the task and the obstacle.

For example, imagine people comparing a particular kind of travel accessory. Existing pages may show attractive prices but make it difficult to establish dimensions, included accessories or delivery availability. The possible gap is the missing decision information, rather than a shortage of product pages.

A useful problem statement follows this pattern: “People trying to do this task struggle because this information or capability is missing.” If the statement needs several unrelated audiences and problems, narrow it before choosing features.

That makes research more concrete. Instead of asking whether someone likes an idea, ask how they currently complete the task, where they get stuck and what they do next.

## Examine the alternatives people already use

A competing website is only one kind of alternative. People may rely on a spreadsheet, a retailer's filters, a forum, a phone call or their own experience. Those substitutes can be effective even if they look less polished than a new product.

Work through the task using the available options. Record where information is missing, where it disagrees and where users must repeat effort. Separate inconvenience from a problem significant enough to change behaviour.

For a hypothetical comparison service, a review might ask:

- Can a visitor identify suitable options without opening many unrelated pages?
- Are measurements and features comparable, or expressed inconsistently?
- Can they tell which offers apply to their location?
- Are important limitations visible before they follow an offer?
- Is the information recent enough for the decision?

A gap becomes more credible when you can show the specific step that existing options fail to support. A different visual style alone is weak evidence of unmet demand.

## Treat search interest as one piece of evidence

Search research can reveal the language people use and the questions that recur. It can also help distinguish a broad topic from a narrower practical need. However, interest in a subject does not automatically mean people need another website about it.

Pair search observations with direct evidence of the task: conversations with potential users, repeated questions, or people trying an early prototype. Listen for what they already do rather than relying only on predictions about what they might use.

There is a useful distinction between attention and intent. Someone reading a definition may want a quick explanation. Someone comparing compatibility details may be close to a decision. A platform should match the task it can serve well instead of treating every visitor as the same kind of customer.

## Test the smallest useful version

Before committing to a large catalogue or a complete application, isolate the assumption that matters most. If the idea depends on clearer comparisons, test a small, accurate comparison. If it depends on a simpler tool, let someone complete one real task with a basic version.

For the travel accessory example, a prototype could compare a handful of fictional or properly sourced items using consistent fields. The purpose would be to observe whether people can identify a suitable option and explain their choice. It would not establish demand for an entire marketplace.

Write down the test question before running the test. “Can a visitor eliminate unsuitable options without help?” is more informative than “Does the page look good?” Record confusion, missing information and abandoned steps alongside successful outcomes.

Choose success criteria appropriate to the task. A useful result may be a completed calculation, a clearer decision or a relevant enquiry. Page views alone do not show that the proposed problem has been solved.

## Check whether the service can be maintained

An idea may solve a real problem and still be difficult to operate. A comparison service needs dependable information. A publishing site needs a way to keep important explanations current. A tool needs maintenance when inputs or requirements change.

The practical questions include where data comes from, whether it may be reused, how corrections reach the page and what happens when a source disappears. Shared technology can reduce some repeated work, but it does not remove these responsibilities.

Commercial viability deserves a separate check. Partner programmes, subscriptions, sales or advertising may support different kinds of service. An available commission is not evidence that the service will be useful, and the highest-paying offer should not define the reader's needs.

## Look at the market you intend to serve

A useful experience in one country may not transfer unchanged to another. Language, currency, product availability, delivery conditions and customer expectations can change what information the reader needs.

For example, showing an overseas offer without explaining its delivery limitations may create more work for a visitor. A regional service should account for the differences that affect the task, rather than simply changing its flag or domain name.

Local commercial and regulatory requirements also need appropriate research before launch. Identifying a market gap is an early planning exercise, not confirmation that a proposed business model is ready to operate everywhere.

## Decide whether to proceed, narrow the idea or stop

A useful research outcome is a decision supported by evidence. Proceed when there is a clear problem, a plausible way to serve it and a manageable way to maintain the service. Narrow the idea when one audience or task shows more promise than the rest. Stop when existing alternatives already work well or the required information cannot be maintained reliably.

A short project brief can capture the audience, current workaround, proposed improvement, evidence from testing and unresolved assumptions. Keeping those points explicit makes later design choices easier to assess.
